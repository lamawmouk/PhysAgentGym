Abstract

Code-reasoning agents trained from rollouts rely on verifiers, such as expert annotation, hand-written unit tests, or learned reward models, each of which needs human effort for every new domain. However, in domains governed by closed-form physical laws, whether the laws themselves can serve as automatic verifiers remains unclear. In this work, we seek to understand whether physical laws can score an agent's simulation without annotation, whether these scores reflect correct physics, and whether they can train small models. We propose PhysAgentGym, an agentic code-execution gym for classical mechanics of point masses, in which a language model writes a Python program that simulates a physics scenario and a rule-based verifier compiles natural-language standards into checks on the emitted trajectory. The scores are annotation-free, since physical laws supply the ground truth and neither the agent nor the verifier reads the exact solution. An audit against these withheld solutions shows that every test trajectory the verifier scores highly, from Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Opus 4.8, GPT‑5, and our trained 7B model, matches the exact solution. Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-Coder at 1.5B, 3B, and 7B on Claude Sonnet 4.5 rollouts selected by the verifier brings the 7B model statistically on par with Sonnet 4.5 and GPT‑5. It matches Sonnet 4.5 on held-out Circular Motion and comes close on held-out Damping, a phenomenon absent from training. Frontier models already reach the highest score the verifier gives in practice, but on harder composite tasks with drag, orbits, and driven and coupled oscillators, where the exact solution earns nearly the full score, the verifier separates correct from wrong physics and Claude Opus 4.8 scores measurably above Sonnet 4.5.

How it works

The laws of motion grade the agent's code

Each task gives the agent a scenario and the rules its motion must obey. The agent writes code that simulates the scenario, and a verifier checks the resulting trajectory against each rule.

1

Task

A scenario in words, its starting state, and rules such as "the object follows a parabolic arc" or "total mechanical energy is conserved".

2

Agent

The model writes Python with NumPy and SciPy, runs it, reads the feedback and revises, for up to six steps. Its output is a trajectory of times and positions.

3

Verifier

Each rule becomes one of 19 physics checks, such as a parabola fit or an energy check, that scores the trajectory from 0 to 1, while the exact solution stays hidden from the agent and the verifier.

4

Training

Claude Sonnet 4.5 rollouts that pass every check with a mean score of at least 0.85 give 491 conversations, on which QLoRA fine-tunes Qwen2.5-Coder at 1.5B, 3B, and 7B.

Results

Small models learn from the verifier’s signal

Untrained Qwen2.5-Coder scores 0.000, 0.011 and 0.668 at 1.5B, 3B and 7B. After training on the 491 verifier-selected rollouts it scores 0.702, 0.888 and 0.942. The 7B model is statistically on par with Claude Sonnet 4.5, and gains 48.7 points on Damping, a phenomenon absent from training.

The 7B model ties with Sonnet 4.5

On the same 120 tasks the 7B model's scores differ from Sonnet's by 0.002 on average, and an equivalence test shows the two agree to within 0.01.

Training is reliable from 3B up

At 1.5B the two training seeds differ by 3.6 points, mostly on Damping, where the 1.5B models produce a parseable action in only 3 to 20 of 30 episodes, whereas the two 3B seeds differ by 0.7 points.

Choosing data by score helps at 1.5B

Keeping only rollouts that also score at least 0.85 adds 12.7 points at 1.5B, while the gains at 3B and 7B, 0.9 and 0.1 points, are not statistically significant.

Verifier audit

Every audited high score is a correct trajectory

Two frontier models agreeing shows the verifier is consistent, not that it is correct, so we checked it against the exact solutions, which neither the agent nor the verifier sees. For the 359 test episodes of Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5, and our 7B model that contain a parseable action, we re-ran the best-scoring code and called a trajectory physically correct if its error is below 5% and it covers at least 95% of the solution's duration.

281 of 281

High scores are correct

All 281 episodes scoring at least 0.9 are physically correct: 94 from Sonnet, 94 from GPT-5 and 93 from the 7B model.

0.12% vs 70%

Correct and wrong sit far apart

Every correct trajectory lies within 0.12% of the exact solution, and every wrong one lies at least 70% away.

51 of 51

Low scores do not always mean wrong physics

All 51 frontier episodes scoring below 0.9 are still correct. Of the 27 such 7B episodes, 7 are wrong, and all 7 score below 0.9.

The low scores on correct trajectories come mostly from the smoothness rule, which marks down Ferris-wheel and constant-acceleration motion, and from the damping rules, so these tolerances, not the agents, cap scores near 0.94. Beyond the audited models, 21 of the 794 high-scoring trajectories of the other trained runs (under 3%) differ from the exact solution.

Harder composite subcategories

Checks that test the laws of motion directly

Four new subcategories, each with 300 train and 30 test tasks: projectile motion with quadratic drag, two-body Kepler orbits, a driven damped oscillator, and two masses coupled by three springs. The scenarios state only the constants and the starting state, so the agent must set up and solve the dynamics. Eleven new checks test Newton's second law, conserved quantities and the stated starting state.

A correct answer earns nearly the full score

The exact solution scores 0.989 to 0.998, against about 0.94 on the original four subcategories.

Wrong physics scores low

Every wrong solution we constructed, such as one that ignores drag or uses a crude integrator, scores 0.76 or less.

Frontier models solve them too

Sonnet 4.5 scores 0.990 and Opus 4.8 scores 0.995, and all 240 submissions are physically correct and score at least 0.9. Opus leads by 0.005 in a paired bootstrap, a measurable gap, while the two tie on the main tasks.

Takeaways

Physical laws are a free source of verification

  • The signal works on its own. It lifts the base models by 70, 88 and 27 points with no human labels, unit tests or reward model.
  • It transfers. The 7B model gains 48.7 points on Damping, a phenomenon absent from training.
  • Scope. The tasks cover classical mechanics of one or two point masses, a small part of physics. The method fits wherever each rule is an exact, computable property of the trajectory, and leaves out chaotic, contact-rich, random and turbulent systems.
